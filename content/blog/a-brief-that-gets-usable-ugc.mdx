When a round of creator content comes back unusable, the instinct is to blame
the casting. Usually the brief is what failed. A creator can only hit a target
they can see.

Here is what a brief has to make unmissable.

## 1. The one thing the video has to land

Not three things. One. If the brief lists five benefits, the creator will try to
fit all five, and the video will land none of them. Pick the single claim, and
state it as the line you'd want a viewer to repeat afterwards.

## 2. The first three seconds, written out

Don't ask a creator to "open with a strong hook." Give two or three hook options
written as actual spoken lines, and let them pick the one that sounds like them.
The point isn't to script the whole video — it's to remove the hardest decision
from the person with the least context.

## 3. What the shot has to show

Be specific and physical: product visible by second two, label facing camera,
natural light, no competitor packaging in frame. These are cheap to state and
expensive to fix in post.

## 4. The non-negotiables, separated from the preferences

Split the brief into **must** and **nice**. Compliance language, claim wording
and legal restrictions are musts. Framing, outfit and location are preferences.
When everything reads as a rule, creators optimise for compliance instead of
performance, and the video goes flat.

## 5. What "done" looks like

Specify deliverables plainly: aspect ratio, length range, whether you need raw
footage, how many hook variants, and the file-naming convention. Ambiguity here
is what turns one round of revisions into three.

## Give them room where it counts

The parts worth protecting are the claim, the shot and the compliance language.
Everything else — pacing, phrasing, personality — is why you hired a creator
rather than a studio. A brief that controls tone usually produces something that
sounds like an ad read, which is precisely what UGC is supposed to avoid.

A good brief fits on one page, and a creator should be able to read it once and
start filming.
