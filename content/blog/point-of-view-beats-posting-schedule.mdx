Most brands we talk to don't have a posting problem. They have a position problem.
The calendar is full, the designer is busy, something ships every weekday — and
none of it is saying anything a competitor couldn't have said instead.

A posting schedule is a logistics answer to a strategy question. It tells you
when a slot needs filling. It never tells you what belongs in the slot.

## Start with the argument, not the format

Before we touch a content calendar, we write down the argument the brand is
making. Not a tagline — an actual claim someone could disagree with. "Most
skincare routines are too complicated" is an argument. "Quality you can trust"
is wallpaper.

The useful test: could a reasonable competitor run the same line tomorrow? If
yes, it isn't a position, it's a category description.

Once the argument exists, formats get easy. A claim people push back on becomes
a comments-bait carousel. A claim that needs proof becomes a demo. A claim that
needs a face becomes a creator script.

## Cadence is a consequence, not an input

We set cadence last, from two things: how much your argument actually has to
say, and how much production you can sustain without the quality sliding.

Posting five times a week with nothing to say is worse than posting twice with
something to say. The feed is not a treadmill you fall off; it's an audience
that learns what to expect from you.

> If removing your logo makes the post indistinguishable from three other
> accounts, the schedule isn't the thing to fix.

## What this looks like in practice

- **One argument per quarter.** Everything laddering up to it, in different
  shapes and at different depths.
- **Three recurring formats, maximum.** Recognition compounds. Novelty resets it.
- **A standing idea list.** Not a calendar of slots, a list of things worth
  saying, ranked. The calendar pulls from the list.
- **A monthly read.** Which arguments landed, which formats carried them, what
  to drop.

The brands that look prolific are rarely the ones producing the most. They're
the ones whose work is recognisable enough that three posts read like thirty.
