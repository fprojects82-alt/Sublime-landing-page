The job of a hook is narrow: make staying slightly more interesting than
scrolling. Not to summarise the video, not to introduce the brand, not to say
hello.

Almost every hook that works does the same structural thing — it opens a gap the
viewer wants closed.

## Six shapes worth stealing

**The contradiction.** State something that shouldn't be true. "The cheapest
option here is also the one we recommend." The viewer stays to find out how both
can hold.

**The named mistake.** "If you're doing this, you're losing half your reach."
Specific enough that someone recognises themselves in it.

**The mid-action open.** Start with hands already doing the thing. No preamble.
Motion buys you a second, and a second is often all you need.

**The stakes-first.** Lead with what it costs to get this wrong, before
explaining what "this" is.

**The direct address.** Name the exact person this is for in the first clause.
A smaller, sharper audience retains far better than a vague large one.

**The receipt.** Open on the proof — the result, the before, the thing in your
hand — and explain afterwards.

## What reliably doesn't work

"Hey guys, so today I wanted to talk about…" — this is a hook that announces a
hook is coming. By the time the actual subject arrives, the viewer has made
their decision.

Same category: logo stings, slow brand animations, and any opening frame that
could belong to any video.

## Write five, film three

Hooks are cheap to write and expensive to guess at. Write five for every video,
film the three that sound natural out loud, and let the data pick. Keep the ones
that work in a running file — hook shapes transfer between topics far more
readily than scripts do.

## Then actually pay it off

A hook that opens a gap and never closes it trains people to distrust the next
one. If you promise a mistake, name the mistake. The hook is a contract, and
retention on the *next* video is what you're really spending.
